**CNT 3004 – Computer Network Concepts** (UCF, Fall 2026)

This course covered how networks are built and how data actually moves across them, from the physical cabling up through the application protocols. Each homework paired written questions with hands-on work using command-line tools and Wireshark. Below is a reflection on each assignment, followed by my thoughts on the quizzes and discussions.

> Lab files, screenshots, and submitted documents are intentionally not posted here. These are reflections only.
{: .prompt-info }

---

## Homework 1 – Networking Fundamentals, Infrastructure & Network Tools (Ch. 1–2)

**Experience**

The first homework was a broad intro to networking. It started with the OSI and TCP/IP models, address sizes (MAC, IPv4, IPv6, ports), topologies, and encapsulation, then moved into physical infrastructure like cabling types, patch panels, and wall jacks. The second half was hands-on: I traced and diagrammed my own network, followed a web request from client to server, and used `ping`, `traceroute`, and Wireshark to look at real traffic leaving my machine.

**Key skills and concepts**
- The 7 OSI layers vs. the TCP/IP model, and which protocols live at each layer (HTTP, TCP, IP, Ethernet)
- The difference between a MAC address (local delivery) and an IP address (end-to-end routing)
- Encapsulation and how each layer wraps the data from the layer above it
- Cable types and best practices for structured cabling
- Using `ping`, `traceroute`, and Wireshark to check connectivity and see the path packets take

**Challenges**

Diagramming my own network was harder than I expected. I use my home network every day but had never thought about where the modem, router, switch, and wireless devices sit in relation to each other. I had to go figure out which devices were doing what before I could draw it accurately.

**Takeaway**

This assignment gave me the vocabulary for the rest of the course. Mapping concepts like encapsulation and the OSI model onto real `traceroute` hops and Wireshark captures made them stick a lot better than memorizing the layers would have.

## Homework 2 – Network Addressing, Protocols & Routing (Ch. 3–4)

**Experience**

This homework focused on addressing and the transport layer. The written portion covered well-known ports, MAC address structure (OUI + NIC identifier), IPv4 vs. IPv6, IP address classes, subnet masks, DHCP, and TCP vs. UDP. I also used tools like `ipconfig`/`ip`, `netstat`, `arp`, and `route` to pull that same information from my own computer. The Wireshark portion had me capture an HTTP GET and 200 OK exchange, break down the TCP three-way handshake, compare UDP DNS queries and responses, and look at DNS A records and their TTL values.

**Key skills and concepts**
- Common port numbers (FTP 21, SMTP 25, HTTP 80, POP3 110) and browsing the IANA port registry
- IPv4 address classes and how a subnet mask separates network and host bits
- APIPA (169.254.x.x) as the fallback when DHCP fails
- Reading the ARP table and the routing table to find the default gateway
- The TCP handshake (SYN, SYN-ACK, ACK), sequence/acknowledgment numbers, and calculating RTT
- How UDP keeps things lightweight compared to TCP, and how DNS uses it on port 53

**Challenges**

Some of the commands were written for Windows and behaved differently (or not at all) on my setup, so I had to find the Linux equivalents and figure out why some values came back empty. Following sequence and acknowledgment numbers across the handshake also took a few tries before it clicked that the ACK is just the other side's sequence number plus one.

**Takeaway**

I came out of this one much more comfortable reading the output of everyday networking commands. Seeing the handshake and DNS lookups happen live in Wireshark turned TCP and UDP from definitions into something I can now recognize in a capture, which carries over directly to the enumeration I do in CTFs.

## Homework 3 – Network Media, Wireless & Throughput (Ch. 5–6)

**Experience**

Homework 3 moved down to the physical and data link layers. The written section covered coax, UTP, and fiber (single-mode vs. multimode), bandwidth vs. throughput, noise/crosstalk/attenuation, simplex/half/full-duplex, multiplexing, straight-through vs. crossover cables, and a lot of wireless material: the spectrum, signal propagation effects, CSMA/CD vs. CSMA/CA, SSID/BSSID/BSS/ESS, and securing Wi-Fi. I also checked the speed and duplex settings on my own adapter. In Wireshark I built an I/O graph, calculated throughput and utilization by hand, and analyzed an 802.11 capture (`wpa-Induction`) to sort management, control, and data frames and pick apart a beacon frame.

**Key skills and concepts**
- Bandwidth (the max capacity) vs. throughput (what you actually get)
- How copper and fiber differ and where each one makes sense
- CSMA/CD for wired Ethernet vs. CSMA/CA for wireless
- The three 802.11 frame types and what a beacon frame advertises (SSID, supported rates, security)
- Calculating throughput, utilization, and Wi-Fi overhead from a real capture

**Challenges**

The 802.11 capture was the toughest part. Wireless frames look nothing like the Ethernet traffic I was used to, and the frame counts did not add up to the total packet count at first, so I had to work out where the missing frames were going. The throughput math also needed care with units (bits vs. bytes, seconds vs. milliseconds).

**Takeaway**

This assignment showed me how much overhead goes into wireless networking that you never see as a user. Breaking down beacon frames was also a good introduction to the 802.11 side of things, which ties into Wi-Fi hacking, one of the topics I want to dig into.

## Homework 4 – Virtualization, Subnetting, VLANs & HTTP (Ch. 7–8)

**Experience**

This homework covered virtualization and cloud (hypervisors, Bridged/NAT/Host-Only networking, IaaS/PaaS/SaaS), remote access protocols (SSH, Telnet, RDP), and a big section on IPv4 classes, default masks, CIDR notation, and subnetting. There was also a VLAN design problem with access and trunk ports between two switches, and I set up an Ubuntu VM in VirtualBox. The Wireshark lab went deep into HTTP: basic GET/response, conditional GETs with `If-Modified-Since` and `304 Not Modified`, large documents split across multiple TCP segments, and pages with embedded objects.

**Key skills and concepts**
- Subnetting by hand: network/broadcast addresses, usable hosts, and slash notation
- VLANs, access ports vs. trunk ports, and why trunks carry tagged traffic between switches
- VM networking modes and when to use each one
- Why SSH replaced Telnet (encryption) and where RDP fits in
- HTTP headers, status codes, browser caching, and how one HTTP response can span many TCP segments

**Challenges**

Subnetting was the part that took the most practice. Getting the block size right and not mixing up the network and broadcast addresses took several problems before it became automatic. On the HTTP lab I had to make sure my browser cache was actually cleared, or the conditional GET results would not show up the way the lab expected.

**Takeaway**

A lot of this connected directly to work I already do. I manage VMs and networks for Hack@UCF and my homelab, and this assignment filled in the theory behind things I had mostly learned by doing, especially VLANs and the VM networking modes. Watching the HTTP caching behavior in Wireshark was also useful for web security, since those headers come up all the time.

## Homework 5 & 6 – Network Management, Security, WAN & TCP Fault Recovery (Ch. 9–12)

**Experience**

The final homework was the widest in scope. I researched security certifications, network risk management tools, and WAN technologies, then used the Microsoft Management Console with Device Manager, Event Viewer, and Services snap-ins. The written section covered security policies, firewalls, switch security, redundancy and Spanning Tree Protocol, MPLS for connecting multiple offices, and performance metrics like latency, jitter, and packet loss. The Wireshark lab had me capture a real file download and then analyze a lossy download capture to find retransmissions, duplicate ACKs, and other TCP recovery events and calculate the retransmission rate.

**Key skills and concepts**
- Security policies, firewall placement, and switch hardening (port security, disabling unused ports)
- Why redundant links need STP to prevent loops
- How MPLS uses labels instead of full routing lookups to forward traffic across a WAN
- Network performance metrics and how they affect users
- Using Wireshark's expert info to find retransmissions, duplicate ACKs, and out-of-order segments, and calculating a retransmission rate

**Challenges**

Separating my own download stream from all the background traffic in the capture took some careful filtering. It was also tricky to explain why the file still arrived complete even though retransmissions happened, until I connected it back to how TCP's acknowledgments guarantee delivery.

**Takeaway**

This assignment pulled together a lot of the course. The security and switch hardening topics line up with the blue team work I do for CCDC, and the TCP fault recovery lab gave me a practical way to tell whether a network problem is actually packet loss or something else.

## Quizzes & Discussions

The quizzes kept me honest about the chapter material. Questions on things like port numbers, IP classes, subnetting, cable types, and the OSI layers forced me to actually memorize details I might have skimmed over while doing the labs. Subnetting and the 802.11 material showed up the most and were the areas where I had to review the most.

The discussions were a good place to connect the course content to real-world situations and see how classmates approached the same topics. Talking through topics like network security and wireless networking with people coming from different backgrounds helped me think about networking from more than just the attacker or defender angle I usually take in competitions.

**Key takeaways from the quizzes and discussions**
- Layered models are the backbone for troubleshooting: figure out which layer is broken first
- Subnetting and addressing are skills that need repetition, not just reading
- Security has to be built into every layer of a network, from switch ports to firewalls to Wi-Fi encryption
