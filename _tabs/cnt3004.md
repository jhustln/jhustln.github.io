**CNT 3004 – Computer Network Concepts** (UCF, Fall 2026)

This course covered how networks are built and how data actually moves across them, from the physical cabling up through the application protocols. Each homework paired written questions with hands-on work using command-line tools and Wireshark. Below is a reflection on each assignment, followed by my thoughts on the quizzes and discussions.



---

## Homework 1 – Networking Fundamentals, Infrastructure & Network Tools (Ch. 1–2)

**Experience**

The first assignment provided an overview of networking. This covered OSI and TCP/IP model, sizes of addresses (MAC, IPv4, IPv6, ports), topologies, and encapsulation, and progressed to physical components such as cables, patch panels, and wall jacks. The latter part was practical in nature, including drawing out my own network topology, following a web request from client to server, and observing real traffic from my computer using `ping`, `traceroute`, and Wireshark.

**Key skills and concepts**
- The 7 OSI layers compared to the TCP/IP protocol suite, and protocols at each layer (HTTP, TCP, IP, Ethernet)
- MAC address (local delivery) versus IP address (route through the end system)
- Encapsulation and how each layer wraps its layer's protocol around the data generated by its next higher layer
- Types of cables and the use of structured cabling
- Use of `ping`, `traceroute`, and Wireshark to verify connectivity and see the path taken by the packets

**Challenges**

Creating a diagram for my personal computer network was more difficult than I thought. While I use my personal computer network on a daily basis, I have not taken the time to think about where the various components, such as the router, modem, switch, and wireless devices, are located.

**Takeaway**

Through this assignment, I was able to get all the terms needed for the whole semester. It really made it easier to understand what each layer does by relating it to how traceroute works and the Wireshark output.

## Homework 2 – Network Addressing, Protocols & Routing (Ch. 3–4)

**Experience**

This assignment was about and addressing the transport layer. The theory part consisted of the study of such topics as known ports, MAC addresses format (OUI + NIC identifier), IPv4 and IPv6, address classes of IP, subnet masks, DHCP, and differences between TCP and UDP. I also used commands such as `ipconfig/ip`, `netstat`, `arp`, and `route` to get the same data about my computer. In Wireshark, I captured the exchange of HTTP GET and 200 OK, analyzed the TCP three-way handshake, and examined UDP DNS requests and replies along with their TTL values.

**Key skills and concepts**
- Common ports (FTP: 21, SMTP: 25, HTTP: 80, POP3: 110), IANA port registration browser
- IP address classes and using subnet mask to segregate network/host part from an IP address
- Use of APIPA (169.254.x.x) in case of DHCP failure
- Check ARP table and routing table to locate default gateway
- TCP 3-way Handshake process, Sequence/Acknowledgement number and RTT computation
- Advantages of UDP over TCP and use of UDP by DNS on port 53

**Challenges**

A number of these commands have been used in Windows and did not work properly (or not at all), therefore, I had to look up the Linux alternatives as well as figure out why certain values returned blank. It took me several times to realize what the ACK was – the sequence number of the other side incremented by one.

**Takeaway**

This time, I feel a lot more at ease with interpreting the output of common networking commands. Actually watching the handshake and DNS lookups take place in Wireshark helped me convert TCP/UDP from definitions to something I now know how to spot in a packet capture, and that translates immediately into enumeration on CTFs.

## Homework 3 – Network Media, Wireless & Throughput (Ch. 5–6)

**Experience**

Homework 3 now fell back to the physical layer and data link layer. The written part included coax, UTP, and fiber optics (single mode versus multimode), bandwidth versus throughput, noise, crosstalk, attenuation, simplex/half/full-duplex, multiplexing, straight-through versus crossover cables, and a bunch of wireless stuff including the spectrum, effects on signal propagation, CSMA/CD versus CSMA/CA, SSID/BSSID/BSS/ESS, and securing Wi-Fi. I also tested the speed and duplex on my own NIC card. In Wireshark, I made an I/O graph, manually computed throughput and utilization, and dissected the 802.11 packet (`wpa-Induction`) to separate management/control/data frames and analyze the beacon frame.

**Key skills and concepts**
- Differences between maximum capacity (bandwidth) and actual capacity (throughput)
- Differences between copper and fiber and where each would be used
- Difference between CSMA/CD in wired Ethernet and CSMA/CA in wireless
- Three different 802.11 frames and what is included in a beacon frame (SSID, speeds, etc.)
- Calculation of throughput, utilization, and Wi-Fi overhead using a packet capture

**Challenges**

The hardest thing was 802.11 capture. There are very big differences between wireless frames and Ethernet frames; I had never worked with wireless frames before, and it was also very confusing that the number of frames didn’t equal the total number of packets at first, but I figured out where those frames went eventually. Throughput calculations required paying attention to units too.

**Takeaway**

This assignment has taught me just how many hidden costs are involved in wireless networking. Dissecting the beacon frames was a great way to start learning about the 802.11 protocol, which is useful for learning more about hacking Wi-Fi, something that interests me.

## Homework 4 – Virtualization, Subnetting, VLANs & HTTP (Ch. 7–8)

**Experience**

The homework included virtualization and cloud computing (hypervisor, Bridged/NAT/Host Only networking, IaaS/PaaS/SaaS), remote access (SSH, Telnet, RDP), and a huge IPv4 classes, default masks, CIDR and subnetting topic. A VLAN configuration question including access and trunk ports of two switches, as well as creating an Ubuntu VM in VirtualBox were also included. The Wireshark assignment analyzed HTTP protocol in detail including GET/response, conditional GETs using `If-Modified-Since` and receiving `304 Not Modified`, large files transmitted through multiple TCP segments, and web pages containing embedded objects.

**Key skills and concepts**
- Subnetting manually: network/broadcast addresses, number of usable hosts, and slash notation
- VLANs, access ports versus trunk ports, and the reason trunks have tagged traffic
- Virtual machine network modes and when to employ each of them
- The reason for switching from Telnet to SSH and the role of RDP
- HTTP header field values, status codes, cache control, and one HTTP response over multiple TCP segments

**Challenges**

The portion of subnetting was what I found most difficult to get a good grasp on. Ensuring the block size was correct and not confusing the network and broadcast address took several questions for me to get into muscle memory. In the HTTP lab, I had to ensure that my browser cache was truly emptied, otherwise the conditional GET wouldn’t work as expected.

**Takeaway**

A lot of this was very much related to what I currently do on a regular basis. My work includes management of virtual machines and networks for Hack@UCF and my own personal lab, and this assignment gave me the theoretical background to some of the things that I've been doing practically until now, such as VLAN and VM network modes. The use of Wireshark to view the HTTP headers was helpful too.

## Homework 5 & 6 – Network Management, Security, WAN & TCP Fault Recovery (Ch. 9–12)

**Experience**

The last homework assignment was the broadest of all. I worked on security certifications, network risk management applications, and WAN solutions and utilized the Microsoft Management Console with the Device Manager, Event Viewer, and Services snap-ins. As for the written part, it entailed security policies, firewalls, switch security, redundancy and the Spanning Tree Protocol, Multi-Protocol Label Switching (MPLS) solution for several offices’ connection, and performance measures such as latency, jitter, and packet loss. The task with Wireshark included capturing an actual file downloading session and analyzing the lossy one in order to identify retransmissions, duplicate ACKs, and other TCP recovery operations.

**Key skills and concepts**
- Policies related to security, firewall configuration, and switch hardening (ports securing, disabling unused ports)
- The reason for having Spanning Tree Protocol on redundant links
- How MPLS forwards packets using labels rather than routing table lookups through the WAN
- Performance measures of the network and their effects on users
- Wireshark expert information and the calculation of the retransmission rate

**Challenges**

Distinguishing my download traffic from all the other background traffic during the packet capture was quite challenging. It was also difficult to understand why the file could still be received intact despite the retransmissions until I realized that it had something to do with how TCP acknowledgments ensure transmission.

**Takeaway**

This project was an interesting combination of a variety of things we learned in the class. The security hardening and switch hardening covered in this class relate to my blue team job at CCDC, while the TCP fault tolerance lab provided me with a useful method of determining whether it is packet loss or not.

## Quizzes & Discussions

The quizzes motivated me to study the topics from the chapters carefully. I was supposed to learn the chapter topics in detail such as port numbers, IP classes, subnets, cables, and the OSI layers. It is evident that I had to study subnetting and 802.11 material quite a lot because of these quizzes.

The discussion sections helped me relate what we learned in the course with the real-life situation. The discussions allowed me to look at networking in various ways apart from being an attacker or the defender as I do in the competitions. In addition, the discussions were helpful when looking at topics like wireless networking and network security.

**Key takeaways from the quizzes and discussions**
- Troubleshooting requires an understanding of the hierarchical model, where you find the faulting layer before anything else.
- Subnetting and addressing require practice, not only theory.
- Security must be integrated into each layer of the network, whether it is port security or Wi-Fi security.
